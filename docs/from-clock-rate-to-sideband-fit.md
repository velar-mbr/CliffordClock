# From one clock rate to a differentiable sideband fit

This document walks through CliffordClock's physical model and its sideband-fitting capability, aimed at a reader who already knows optical-clock systematics well and wants the underlying ideas without wading through the full formalism. It follows the order a reader would naturally ask these questions in: what is the model, why does a budget table work the way it does, what does a Born-Oppenheimer+WKB (BO+WKB) sideband fit count? What does automatic differentiation buy, why has this combination not shown up in the literature before, and what should a reader read to go deeper?

## One scalar per atom

CliffordClock's physical model reduces to one idea: each atom in the clock carries a single number, its clock rate, and every systematic effect enters as a multiplicative correction to that number. Blackbody radiation, a stray DC field, the lattice light itself, motion through the trap, the electric quadrupole shift, and the local gravitational potential each shift that rate by a small fraction, evaluated at the atom's own position, velocity, and quantum state at that instant. Multiply the bare transition frequency by every one of those corrections in turn, and integrate the result along an atom's own path through the interrogation, and the outcome is the accumulated phase a Ramsey or Rabi measurement reads.

Relativity already has a name for a number that scales an ideal clock's rate this way. In a 3+1 decomposition of spacetime it is called the lapse function; for an observer standing still in a gravitational field it is the redshift factor, the square root of minus the time-time metric component. CliffordClock calls the product of every correction the pivot, and each individual correction a pivot perturbation. The vocabulary differs by community, but the object underneath is the same ratio: an atom's own local clock rate divided by a freely running reference rate.

GPS is the clearest place to see that ratio already at work, because it is a system whose numbers a reader already trusts. Ashby's 2003 review works out a GPS satellite clock's own rate, relative to a ground clock, as a product of a gravitational-potential term and a special-relativistic velocity term, multiplied together along the satellite's own worldline. That product is the same proper-time-rate object CliffordClock composes for an atomic clock's systematics, with two terms in place of six.

Chronometric geodesy runs the same idea backward. An optical clock's measured rate, compared against a reference, becomes a measurement of the gravitational potential difference between the clock and the reference: read the rate, and infer the height. Mehlstäubler et al.'s 2018 review of atomic clocks for geodesy surveys this practice across a range of clock platforms, each one treating a clock's fractional frequency shift as a rate factor tied to the local metric.

CliffordClock's own composition rule fits in six lines of code, coordinated word for word with the methods paper it accompanies. Every physical model above enters as one term in the loop below; the rate is their product with the bare frequency, the ensemble's mean phase gives the reported shift, and the modulus of the mean phasor gives the Ramsey fringe visibility:

```python
def clock_phase(worldline, terms, nu_0):
    rate = lambda t: nu_0 * prod(1.0 + p(worldline.state(t)) for p in terms)
    return 2.0 * pi * integrate(rate, worldline.proper_time)

phases = [clock_phase(w, terms, NU_0) for w in ensemble]
shift = mean(phases) / (2.0 * pi * NU_0 * T) - 1.0
visibility = abs(mean(exp(1j * array(phases))))
```

The full context for this listing lives in [`docs/MODEL.md`](MODEL.md), where it is checked to match the companion paper's own printed version.

## Why the budget adds

A conventional systematic budget lists one row per effect and adds the rows to a total fractional shift. That addition is not a separate approximation layered on top of the pivot picture above. It falls out of the same product, kept to first order.

Expanding the product of every `(1 + p_k)` term gives `1 + Σ p_k + Σ_{j<k} p_j p_k + …`. The additive table a budget prints is exactly the first sum, the linear term; everything after it is a cross term the table drops.

CliffordClock's own documentation computes two of those cross terms directly, each with its own stated bound. The DC-Stark shift and blackbody radiation are independent perturbations of an atom's internal energy, and their second-order cross term averages to exactly zero in an isotropic thermal field; the next surviving cross term is bounded near `1e-33`. The gravitational redshift depends only on an atom's position, never on its motional or internal state, so its cross term with any field-driven shift is bounded near `1e-31`. Both bounds sit at least twelve orders of magnitude below the `1e-18`-to-`1e-19` systematic floor a modern optical clock reports.

The point of stating these bounds is structural. No published budget total changes because of them. The bounds give the table's own linear arithmetic a stated reason to work as well as it does, and give it a stated numerical boundary, in trap depth and coupling strength, for where it would stop working.

## What BO+WKB counts, and how a sideband forms

Two laser beams meet head-on and build a standing wave, whose intensity peaks form a row of potential wells that one atom sits inside. Along the beam axis, the well follows a squared cosine: it curves like a parabola near the bottom, but unlike a parabola it has a finite depth and flattens out again near the top. Across the beam, a Gaussian intensity profile confines the atom more loosely, and that confinement weakens as the atom's radial motion carries it toward the beam's edge.

The harmonic approximation replaces the true well with the parabola it resembles near the bottom, and for the lowest levels the two nearly agree. Beloy et al.'s BO+WKB model keeps the true squared-cosine well instead. It solves the atom's motion along the beam axis at each radius directly, a Born-Oppenheimer separation, then applies a WKB approximation across the beam to count how many radial motional levels sit at a given energy. That count is the density of states, and it is where the two models part ways. A parabola's density of states grows in a fixed, simple way as energy rises. The true well's flattening geometry opens up radial room faster, so its density of states grows faster too, and the gap widens toward the top of the band.

CliffordClock's own notebook on the lattice light shift measures that gap directly. At one representative trap depth, the true model's cumulative radial state count runs from about 5 percent above the harmonic prediction at the coldest radial temperature checked to about 12 percent above it at the warmest. A colder atom's motion stays close to the band bottom, where the two models nearly agree; a warmer atom samples more of the flattening tail, where they do not.

A sideband is how that level structure becomes visible to a probe beam. A scan across the clock transition sees a carrier at the bare frequency, plus a blue and a red sideband shifted by close to the trap's own axial vibration frequency. The blue sideband's detuning traces back to the energy gap between two axial bands at the radius the atom's own energy allows it to reach, its Franck-Condon point. The sideband's shape traces back to how many radial states the atom's thermal population samples on the way there. The true well admits more radial states than the harmonic one at the same energy. Its sideband spreads its weight across a wider range of detunings as a result, and peaks at a measurably lower detuning than the harmonic model predicts at matched trap depth and temperature. CliffordClock's own sideband-fitting notebook computes that difference directly, for both models side by side.

## What differentiable means here

Automatic differentiation computes an exact derivative of a program, built up by mechanically applying the chain rule to every elementary operation the program executes at runtime, at a cost close to the cost of running the program itself. Baydin et al.'s 2018 survey draws two boundaries around what that means. A finite-difference approximation perturbs an input and measures the resulting change; automatic differentiation computes the same derivative exactly, with no perturbation involved. Symbolic differentiation manipulates a formula on paper; automatic differentiation runs the actual program and accumulates derivatives alongside its ordinary computation.

CliffordClock's BO+WKB sideband spectrum is such a program, built in JAX, so it can be differentiated with respect to trap depth and radial temperature at machine cost. Supplying `jax.value_and_grad` of that forward model, together with a measured or synthetic spectrum, to `scipy.optimize.minimize` turns the model into a fitting tool. Gradient descent walks trap depth and radial temperature toward the values that best explain the scan, using exact gradients through the whole physics chain: the axial eigensolve, the WKB density of states, and the thermal average, all at once.

CliffordClock's own fitting grid puts a number on what that buys. Fitting a synthetic spectrum back with the BO+WKB path recovers the radial temperature with an uncertainty 2 to 13.5 times tighter than fitting the same spectrum with the harmonic model, across the grid points where both fits report a valid uncertainty. That tighter recovery is a direct consequence of the wider, better-resolved level structure the previous section describes: a model that correctly counts more of the available radial states constrains the temperature that produced them more tightly.

A fit's reported uncertainty is only trustworthy when the mathematics behind it holds. CliffordClock computes each fit's one-sigma uncertainty from the Hessian of the negative log-likelihood at the optimum, the Laplace approximation, which requires every eigenvalue of that Hessian to be positive. The code checks those eigenvalues directly before it ever inverts the Hessian into a covariance matrix. Across a twelve-case grid spanning both lineshape models, three truth points, and two noise seeds, eleven fits land within two standard deviations of truth, and the twelfth stops at a saddle point: `L-BFGS-B` reports success, but the Hessian there carries one negative eigenvalue. CliffordClock reports that row's uncertainty as `nan`. An earlier version of the same code clamped the invalid, negative variance to zero and printed a small, confident-looking number there, for the row with the least trustworthy result in the whole table. The eigenvalue check now catches that failure mode directly, before a reader ever sees it.

## Why this had not been assembled

Two literatures each hold half of what this document describes, and they do not routinely cite each other. Relativistic timing, the community built around GPS and chronometric geodesy, has composed rate scalars for gravitational and kinematic terms for decades. Atomic-clock systematic-budget practice is a separate literature with its own conventions, built around a published formula for each named effect, summed as independent rows.

A search across both literatures for a published bridge, a paper or textbook chapter that connects relativistic rate-scalar composition explicitly to atomic-clock systematic-budget practice, turned up general-relativity-and-clocks reviews that cover the physics and general clock-systematics reviews that list and bound the terms. It did not turn up a text that frames the two together: here is why independent small perturbations add inside one rate scalar, and here is the bound on when they would stop adding. We did not find that bridge in the published literature.

The same split shows up in software. One general-purpose, open-source implementation of the BO+WKB motional-spectrum problem is public: large-lattice-model, built and maintained at INRIM, Italy's national metrology institute. Its own origin paper is Goti, Condio, Clivati, Risaro, Gozzelino, Costanzo, Levi, Calonico, and Pizzocaro's 2023 Metrologia paper on an absolute frequency measurement of a ytterbium optical clock. A later paper with an overlapping author list, Goti et al. 2025, is a separate work that uses that same package as its reference implementation for fitting real spectroscopy. large-lattice-model's own fitter, `get_fit_sidebands`, pairs a forward model with a finite-difference Jacobian, the numerical route Goti et al. 2025 used against real sideband scans from IT-Yb1, INRIM's own ytterbium lattice clock.

CliffordClock's own sideband fit supplies its optimizer with exact gradients of a BO+WKB sideband lineshape, computed by automatic differentiation through the full physics chain, in place of a finite-difference Jacobian's separately perturbed evaluations. We searched the published literature for a gradient-based fit of this lineshape class and did not find one. What already exists, and is credited above by name, is a working, finite-difference fit of the same model class, already used against real data. CliffordClock adds a differentiable version of the same forward model, one that supplies an optimizer with exact gradients in place of finite-difference approximations to them.

## What to read, in order

The seventeen readings below build in one direction: from a system whose numbers a reader already trusts, through the budget practice a reader already runs, to CliffordClock's own equations, code, and notebooks. Only large-lattice-model's own origin paper sits fully behind a journal paywall, with a green open-access author copy from the authors' own institution.
