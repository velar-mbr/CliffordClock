# One rate per atom: the ideas behind CliffordClock, and what to read

An atom held in an optical clock carries its own internal clock: a transition between two energy levels, ticking at a fixed bare frequency. Six physical effects nudge that tick rate away from the bare value, each by a small fraction: blackbody radiation from the surrounding chamber walls, a stray DC electric field, the lattice light that traps the atom, the atom's own motion through the trap, the electric quadrupole shift, and the local gravitational potential. Each nudge is evaluated at the atom's own position, velocity, and quantum state at that instant, so the same atom feels a different combination of corrections as it moves and evolves through the interrogation. These ideas build in one direction: what the scalar model is, why a conventional budget table's addition is the right first-order approximation to it, how the same per-atom phases give both the reported shift and the Ramsey fringe visibility, what a Born-Oppenheimer+WKB (BO+WKB) sideband fit counts that a harmonic fit misses, how the same composition rule closes a trapped-ion motional budget, what differentiability buys a sideband fit, why this combination has not appeared in the literature before, and where to read further.

## One scalar per atom

CliffordClock's physical model reduces all six effects to one idea: each atom carries a single number, its clock rate, and every one of those six effects enters as a multiplicative correction to that number. Multiply the bare transition frequency by every correction in turn, and integrate the result along the atom's own path through the interrogation, and the outcome is the accumulated phase a Ramsey or Rabi measurement reads.

Relativity already has a name for a number that scales an ideal clock's rate this way. Split spacetime into a stack of spatial snapshots advancing in time, and the factor converting one tick of the stack's own time coordinate into one tick of proper time is called the lapse function; for an observer standing still in a gravitational field the same ratio is called the redshift factor, the square root of minus the time-time metric component. CliffordClock calls the product of every correction the pivot, and each individual correction a pivot perturbation. The vocabulary differs by community, but the object underneath is the same ratio: an atom's own local clock rate divided by a freely running reference rate.

GPS is the clearest place to see that ratio already at work, because it is a system whose numbers a reader already trusts. Ashby's 2003 review works out a GPS satellite clock's own rate, relative to a ground clock, as a product of a gravitational-potential term and a special-relativistic velocity term, multiplied together along the satellite's own worldline. That product is the same proper-time-rate object CliffordClock composes for an atomic clock's systematics: GPS's own two terms, the gravitational-potential factor and the special-relativistic velocity factor, correspond to two of CliffordClock's own six, the gravitational redshift and the motional time-dilation correction. CliffordClock's other four terms, blackbody radiation, the DC-Stark shift, the lattice light shift, and the electric quadrupole shift, correct for a trapped atom's own local field environment. A free-flying GPS satellite's orbit carries no such fields to correct for.

Chronometric geodesy runs the same idea backward. An optical clock's measured rate, compared against a reference, becomes a measurement of the gravitational potential difference between the clock and the reference: read the rate, and infer the height. Mehlstäubler et al.'s 2018 review of atomic clocks for geodesy surveys this practice across a range of clock platforms, each one treating a clock's fractional frequency shift as a rate factor tied to the local metric.

CliffordClock's own composition rule fits in six lines of code. Every physical model above enters as one term in the loop below; the rate is their product with the bare frequency, the ensemble's mean phase gives the reported shift, and the modulus of the mean phasor gives the Ramsey fringe visibility:

```python
def clock_phase(worldline, terms, nu_0):
    rate = lambda t: nu_0 * prod(1.0 + p(worldline.state(t)) for p in terms)
    return 2.0 * pi * integrate(rate, worldline.proper_time)

phases = [clock_phase(w, terms, NU_0) for w in ensemble]
shift = mean(phases) / (2.0 * pi * NU_0 * T) - 1.0
visibility = abs(mean(exp(1j * array(phases))))
```

The full context for this listing lives in [`docs/MODEL.md`](MODEL.md), which is checked to match the listing printed in Section VI of the companion paper, [`paper/composition/main.pdf`](../paper/composition/main.pdf).

## Why the budget adds

A conventional systematic budget lists one row per effect and adds the rows to a total fractional shift. The pivot's own product, `prod(1+p_k)`, becomes that row-by-row sum once it is expanded and truncated at first order.

Multiply out `prod_k (1 + p_k)` for however many terms the model carries, and three kinds of term appear. Multiplying every factor's own `1` together gives a constant `1`. Picking one `p_k` from its own factor and every other factor's `1` gives the linear sum, `Σ_k p_k`. Picking two or more `p`'s from two or more different factors at once gives the cross terms, `Σ_{j<k} p_j p_k` at second order and higher products at third order and beyond. A budget table's addition keeps the constant and the linear sum; every cross term is the part it drops.

CliffordClock's own documentation bounds three specific cross terms among the ones a linear sum drops. The DC-Stark shift and blackbody radiation are independent perturbations of an atom's internal energy; their second-order cross term averages to exactly zero in an isotropic thermal field, because a thermal bath's mean electric field vanishes as a vector. A fourth-order hyperpolarizability cross term between the same two shifts survives that averaging, and the linear sum drops it too; it is bounded near `1e-22`, about four orders of magnitude below the `1e-18` systematic floor a modern optical clock reports. A third correction, the direct product of the two shifts, `(P-1)_stark * (P-1)_BBR`, is bounded near `1e-33`. The gravitational redshift depends only on an atom's position, never on its motional or internal state, so its cross term with any field-driven shift is bounded near `1e-31`. Both the product bound and this gravitational bound sit at least twelve orders of magnitude below the same floor. Each of these bounds is an analytic estimate: a perturbation-theory scaling relation evaluated at the magnitudes real setups publish, the size of each budget row, the published hyperpolarizability shift scale, and a laboratory-scale height difference. The bounds move with a specific clock's operating conditions, so a stray field ten times larger grows the Stark-related bounds in proportion. The margins carry the conclusion: the largest bound sits four orders of magnitude below the systematic floor, and realistic changes in operating conditions move these estimates by far less than that gap.

The literature's published budget totals lose nothing measurable by keeping only the linear sum: every cross term this section bounds sits at least four orders of magnitude below the floor those totals report. The product form's own value is bookkeeping: it gives the table's linear arithmetic a stated reason to work as well as it does, and a stated numerical boundary, in trap depth and coupling strength, for where it would stop working.

## One calculation, two observables

Each atom in the ensemble accumulates its own phase along its own trajectory. The phase integrates the rate CliffordClock's composition rule defines, the bare frequency times the pivot, evaluated at that atom's own position, velocity, and quantum state as they evolve through the interrogation. Two atoms on different paths through the same trap therefore end the interrogation with different accumulated phases.

The ensemble's mean phase, divided by the bare oscillation over the same interrogation time, gives the fractional frequency shift a budget table reports. The same set of per-atom phases, treated as unit vectors on the complex plane and averaged, gives a second quantity from that identical phase draw: the length of the resulting mean vector is the Ramsey fringe visibility. Phases that stay aligned average to a vector close to length one; phases spread by the ensemble's own spread in position and velocity average to something shorter. The shift and the visibility come out of the same accumulated-phase distribution, with no separate calculation and no free parameter of its own.

For a Gaussian-distributed motional state, thermal, coherent, or squeezed, the mean phasor's length reduces to a closed form: `V = exp(-σ_Φ²/2)`, with `σ_Φ²` the accumulated-phase distribution's variance. CliffordClock checks that closed form directly. A pipeline run of a classical worldline ensemble at 4000 worldlines, swept across five squeezing values, compares the pipeline's own reported visibility against the closed form computed independently from the same run's accumulated phases. The largest deviation between the two, at the largest squeezing swept, is about 0.65%, consistent with the `O(σ⁴/N)` Monte Carlo scaling the project's own test-tolerance derivation documents for that comparison at that sample size.

## What BO+WKB counts, and how a sideband forms

Two laser beams meet head-on and build a standing wave, whose intensity peaks form a row of potential wells that one atom sits inside. Along the beam axis, the well follows a squared cosine: it curves like a parabola near the bottom, but unlike a parabola it has a finite depth and flattens out again near the top. Across the beam, a Gaussian intensity profile confines the atom more loosely, and that confinement weakens as the atom's radial motion carries it toward the beam's edge.

The harmonic approximation replaces the true well with the parabola it resembles near the bottom, and for the lowest levels the two nearly agree. Beloy et al.'s BO+WKB model keeps the true squared-cosine well instead. It solves the atom's motion along the beam axis at each radius directly, a Born-Oppenheimer separation, then applies a WKB approximation across the beam to count how many radial motional levels sit at a given energy. That count is the density of states, and it is where the two models part ways. A parabola's density of states grows in a fixed, simple way as energy rises. The true well's flattening geometry opens up radial room faster, so its density of states grows faster too, and the gap widens toward the top of the band.

CliffordClock's own notebook on the lattice light shift measures that gap directly. At one representative trap depth, the true model's cumulative radial state count runs from about 5 percent above the harmonic prediction at the coldest radial temperature checked to about 12 percent above it at the warmest. A colder atom's motion stays close to the band bottom, where the two models nearly agree; a warmer atom samples more of the flattening tail, where they do not.

A sideband is how that level structure becomes visible to a probe beam. A scan across the clock transition sees a carrier at the bare frequency, plus a blue and a red sideband shifted by close to the trap's own axial vibration frequency. The blue sideband's detuning traces back to the energy gap between two axial bands at the radius the atom's own energy allows it to reach, its Franck-Condon point. The sideband's shape traces back to how many radial states the atom's thermal population samples on the way there. The true well admits more radial states than the harmonic one at the same energy. Its sideband spreads its weight across a wider range of detunings as a result, and peaks at a measurably lower detuning than the harmonic model predicts at matched trap depth and temperature. CliffordClock's own sideband-fitting notebook computes that difference directly, for both models side by side.

## The same composition rule on a trapped ion

A trapped two-ion clock's motional time-dilation shift depends on two physically distinct pieces of the same underlying vibration. One piece is how much of a shared normal mode the clock ion itself carries, its participation; a two-ion crystal's modes are collective, so each ion carries only part of a given mode's motion. The other piece is how much extra oscillation, at the trap's own radio-frequency drive, that secular motion itself induces, its intrinsic micromotion. CliffordClock composes both pieces as two multiplicative factors on the same per-mode rate, the same pivot-product rule that composes every other systematic in this model.

Marshall et al.'s published 27Al+/25Mg+ two-ion crystal is a real case where composing only one of the two pieces goes badly wrong before it goes right. A single-species-mass shortcut, one atomic mass carried through every mode with no participation split and no micromotion correction, lands inside the crystal's published secular-motion band, at 0.10 sigma. That agreement is a near-cancellation of two large omissions moving in opposite directions: a clock ion's true participation in a shared mode is close to one-half, and the true micromotion enhancement on the radial modes is close to a factor of two, so the shortcut's implicit choice of one for both factors happens to land close to their true product. Correcting only the participation piece, with the micromotion factor still missing, exposes that accident directly: the total under-predicts the published band by close to a factor of two, landing 14 sigma from it.

Composing the intrinsic-micromotion enhancement back in as a second factor on the same per-mode rate closes most of that distance. Solving the two ions' coupled equations of motion directly, then fitting the resulting trap model to all four measured radial mode frequencies at once, closes the rest: the total lands 0.08 sigma from Marshall et al.'s published band, using only their published mode frequencies and occupations as input. The single-mass shortcut's own 0.10-sigma agreement is a coincidence of the approximation. The coupled two-ion composition is what accounts for the published per-mode physics directly.

Both totals are classified as arithmetic reproduction, computed directly from Marshall et al.'s published inputs. `notebooks/13_trapped_ion_quantum_motion.ipynb` walks the full six-step progression between them, mode by mode.

## What differentiable means here

Automatic differentiation computes an exact derivative of a program, built up by mechanically applying the chain rule to every elementary operation the program executes at runtime, at a cost close to the cost of running the program itself. Baydin et al.'s 2018 survey draws two boundaries around what that means. A finite-difference approximation perturbs an input and measures the resulting change; automatic differentiation computes the same derivative exactly, with no perturbation involved. Symbolic differentiation manipulates a formula on paper; automatic differentiation runs the actual program and accumulates derivatives alongside its ordinary computation.

CliffordClock's BO+WKB sideband spectrum is such a program, built in JAX, so it can be differentiated with respect to trap depth and radial temperature at machine cost. Supplying `jax.value_and_grad` of that forward model, together with a measured or synthetic spectrum, to `scipy.optimize.minimize` turns the model into a fitting tool. Gradient descent walks trap depth and radial temperature toward the values that best explain the scan, using exact gradients through the whole physics chain: the axial eigensolve, the WKB density of states, and the thermal average, all at once.

CliffordClock's own fitting grid measures how much that wider level structure improves a temperature fit. Fitting a synthetic spectrum back with the BO+WKB path recovers the radial temperature with an uncertainty 2 to 13.5 times tighter than fitting the same spectrum with the harmonic model, across the grid points where both fits report a valid uncertainty. That tighter recovery is a direct consequence of the wider, better-resolved level structure the previous section describes: a model that correctly counts more of the available radial states constrains the temperature that produced them more tightly.

A fit's reported uncertainty is only trustworthy when the mathematics behind it holds. CliffordClock computes each fit's one-sigma uncertainty from the Hessian of the negative log-likelihood at the optimum, the Laplace approximation, which requires every eigenvalue of that Hessian to be positive. The code checks those eigenvalues directly before it ever inverts the Hessian into a covariance matrix. Across a twelve-case grid spanning both lineshape models, three truth points, and two noise seeds, eleven fits land within two standard deviations of truth, and the twelfth stops at a saddle point: `L-BFGS-B` reports success, but the Hessian there carries one negative eigenvalue. The eigenvalue check catches this directly and blocks the inversion; CliffordClock reports that row's uncertainty as `nan`, the correct output when the Laplace approximation's own precondition fails.

## Why this had not been assembled

Relativistic timing and atomic-clock systematic-budget practice are two literatures that do not routinely cite each other. The first, the community built around GPS and chronometric geodesy, has composed rate scalars for gravitational and kinematic terms for decades. The second is atomic-clock systematic-budget practice, a separate literature with its own conventions, built around a published formula for each named effect, summed as independent rows.

We searched both literatures for a published bridge: a paper or textbook chapter connecting relativistic rate-scalar composition explicitly to atomic-clock systematic-budget practice. Each side already has its own literature: general-relativity-and-clocks reviews that cover the physics, and general clock-systematics reviews that list and bound the terms. Neither supplies why independent small perturbations add inside one rate scalar, or the bound on when they stop adding.

CliffordClock checks its own BO+WKB solver against one independent, open-source implementation of the same motional-spectrum problem: large-lattice-model, built and maintained at INRIM, Italy's national metrology institute. large-lattice-model solves the same eigenproblem with exact Mathieu-function characteristic values; CliffordClock's own solver uses finite differences. The two methods agree to `1.06e-7` relative error on the band-bottom eigenvalues and `4.7e-3` on the Franck-Condon detunings the sideband model uses. That cross-validation is recorded in `docs/CONVENTIONS.md` section 18 (Deliverable 2). large-lattice-model's own origin paper is Goti, Condio, Clivati, Risaro, Gozzelino, Costanzo, Levi, Calonico, and Pizzocaro's 2023 Metrologia paper on an absolute frequency measurement of a ytterbium optical clock. A later, separate paper with an overlapping author list, Goti et al. 2025, used that same package to fit real sideband scans from IT-Yb1, INRIM's own ytterbium lattice clock.

The practical difference for a user is what each package hands an optimizer. large-lattice-model's own `fit.py` supplies `get_fit_sidebands`, a factory returning a forward model reduced to four fit parameters, meant to be handed to an external least-squares routine. It carries no analytic gradient of its own. A fit built on it therefore differentiates numerically: the optimizer builds its Jacobian, the matrix of parameter derivatives a fit needs, by perturbing each parameter in turn. That is the route Goti et al. 2025 used. CliffordClock's own sideband fit supplies its optimizer with exact gradients of the same model class, computed by automatic differentiation through the full physics chain: the axial eigensolve, the WKB density of states, and the thermal average, all at once. We searched the published literature for a gradient-based fit of this lineshape class and did not find one; large-lattice-model's own finite-difference fit, credited above, is the closest existing work, already used against real data.
